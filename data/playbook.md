# Playbook

The plan is forty weeks. This is the part that decides whether you finish it.

## What the goal is really worth

1:20 means holding 3:47/km for 21.1 km. Your 2019 best of 1:25:18 was 4:02/km, which is worth roughly 38:40 for 10 km. So the A goal is not a comeback — it is a lifetime personal best by five minutes and eighteen seconds, seven years later, on a body with a two-year shin splint history, heavy work travel and two contact sports.

That reframe cuts both ways. In your favour: you have run 1:25 before, the engine has been there, and aerobic memory is real. Against you: nobody accidentally takes 6% off a half marathon PB. It takes the biggest and most consistent volume block you have ever done, and it takes staying healthy through all of it.

- **A goal 1:20** — live only if the shins stay green and the winter runs uninterrupted.
- **B goal 1:22–1:24** — a clear PB, and the most likely honest outcome of a clean build.
- **C goal 1:25** — matching 2019 at an older age, which is its own kind of win.
- **The real one: arrive at the start line healthy.** The thing most likely to cost you 1:20 is not a missed session. It is six weeks off in February.

**Volume escalation clause.** The plan peaks at 72 km. If you reach week 24 with every week green and no amber since December, add 5% to weeks 27 through 35 — three or four km each — and let the peak run to 78. If there has been any amber, do not.

## The traffic light that overrules everything

**Green.** No shin pain during the run, after it, or the next morning. Progress exactly as written.

**Amber.** Mild diffuse ache that settles within 24 hours. Repeat the week rather than adding to it, cut one hard session, keep the calf work. Two clear weeks before you progress again.

**Red.** Pain during the run, pain that changes how you run, night pain, or tenderness you can cover with two fingertips on the bone. Stop running. Cross-train, keep lifting, see a physio. Focal bone tenderness is treated as a bone stress injury until imaging says otherwise.

The load principle from the bone stress injury literature: the right dose is the one that produces no symptoms during, after, or the day following. Distance progresses ahead of speed, and the 10% rule is a rough habit rather than a law.

## Six rules built into the plan

- **Frequency before distance, distance before speed.** Weeks 1–6 add running days and easy kilometres. Nothing faster than a 20-second stride until the week 6 test.
- **Two hard days a week, three at most counting sport.** A hockey game is a hard day. If a weekend has both a game and floorball, you get one hard running session that week.
- **Every fourth week comes down.** Weeks 4, 10, 16, 23, 26, 31 and 36 drop volume. Bone adapts in the gaps, not in the sessions.
- **Soft surfaces early.** Gravel and forest track for easy running through the rebuild. Save asphalt for quality sessions where you need the pace feedback.
- **Rotate and retire shoes.** Two pairs alternating, both replaced by 600–800 km. Log the day you open a pair.
- **Never make up missed kilometres.** A missed week is gone. Rejoin the plan where it stands; if you have lost more than ten days, repeat your last completed week.

## Strength, twice a week, non-negotiable

This has the strongest evidence behind it of anything in the plan, both for your shins and for your race time. Lift after runs or on separate days, never before a key session.

**Session A — heavy.** Trap-bar deadlift or back squat 4×4–6 at roughly 80–85% effort · single-leg Romanian deadlift 3×8 · standing calf raise 3×8 loaded with slow lowering · Copenhagen plank 3×20 s each side.

**Session B — calf, foot and spring.** Seated calf raise 3×10–12 with a bent knee, which loads the soleus, the muscle that takes load off your tibia · split squat 3×8 each leg · tibialis raise 3×15 · pogo hops building from 2×20 to 4×30 contacts across the plan · single-leg hops once you are pain-free for three weeks.

Meta-analyses find heavy resistance training at loads at or above 80% of one-rep max improves running economy more than plyometrics alone, with the largest effects for runners moving at faster speeds, and combining heavy lifting with jump training beats either on its own. A 2025 meta-analysis of shin splint prevention trials found neuromuscular training reduced injury odds with high certainty. Static stretching did nothing.

One more finding worth acting on: shock-absorbing insoles did not prevent shin splints in the pooled trials, while insoles that control overpronation did. If your feet roll in noticeably, that is a physio conversation, not a shoe-shop upsell.

## Form work: cadence, and only cadence

Do not rebuild your gait from scratch. Change one variable. Count your cadence on an easy run, then spend 2×10 minutes twice a week running 5–8% quicker over the ground at the same effort, with a metronome app or a playlist at the right tempo. Shorter, quicker steps land the foot closer under you, which is what lowers the load rate through the tibia.

Expect it to feel awkward and slightly harder for the first few weeks. Add strides on top: 20 seconds, relaxed and tall, full recovery. Strides buy speed and mechanics at almost no bone cost, which is exactly what someone with your history wants.

Reviews of step-rate manipulation consistently show a 5–10% increase reduces vertical loading rates and tibial load, and a 12-week retraining trial held the change. The honest caveat: injury-outcome evidence is thin and increased perceived effort is documented. It is a cheap intervention with a plausible mechanism, not a guarantee.

## Travel weeks

Flip the travel switch on any week you are away and the plan shrinks to about 45% volume: one intensity session, one longer easy run, an optional third run, and ten minutes of hotel strength twice.

The evidence here is reassuring. Endurance fitness holds for weeks on two sessions a week, or on a third to two-thirds of your usual volume, **as long as intensity is preserved**. Cutting intensity is what costs you fitness. Cutting volume mostly costs you nothing over a week or two. So on a bad travel week, protect the hard session and let the easy kilometres go.

Plan the calendar rather than reacting to it. Put known trips on the travel switch now, and where you can, line a trip up with a scheduled down week — 4, 10, 16, 23, 26, 31, 36. A trip that lands on a down week costs almost nothing.

## Hockey and floorball

Skating is genuinely useful: aerobic work with very little tibial load. Floorball is the opposite — hard indoor floor, cutting, stopping and sprinting, on legs with a shin history. Treat floorball as a hard session, not as fun that comes free.

Three weekend shapes, pick the one that matches the fixture:

- **Game Friday night** — no running Friday, long run late Saturday morning, Sunday easy plus floorball.
- **Game Saturday daytime** — Friday off, game Saturday, long run Sunday morning, skip floorball.
- **Game Sunday** — long run Saturday, game Sunday, skip floorball.

From week 38, stop both. Three weeks without cutting sports before a goal race is cheap insurance. A rolled ankle in the second week of May ends the project.

## Testing calendar

| Week | When | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | late Sep | 3 km time trial — sets your first real paces |
| 13 | Nov | 5 km time trial or parkrun |
| 18 | mid Dec | 5 km race effort before the holidays |
| 28 | late Feb | **10 km — the decision gate** |
| 33 | late Mar | 5 km race |
| 36 | mid Apr | 10 km race — final calibration |

Week 28 is measured against 2019, when your 1:25:18 was worth about 38:40 for 10 km. Under 37:30 and 1:20 stays live. 38:00–38:40 means aim at 1:22–1:23. Slower than 38:40 means the honest target is a PB rather than a round number, and you race accordingly instead of blowing up at 15 km.

Set race-day pace from week 36, not from hope.

## The one thing that decides this

Across elite distance runners, the volume of easy running is the strongest single predictor of long-distance performance, with tempo runs and short intervals as the quality that matters. This plan reflects that: roughly 80% of the kilometres are easy, the hard work is concentrated, and the winter is built pyramidal — mostly easy, meaningful threshold, a little fast — before shifting more polarized in the specific phase, which is the pattern that holds up in studies of events from 800 m through the half marathon.

Which means the way you fail is not by training too gently. It is by running your easy days at 4:40 because they feel fine.
